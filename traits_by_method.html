Orbital features by discovery method

These faceted histograms show the distribution of results of orbital traits for each discovery method. With this, it becomes obvious that the majority of planets have relatively low values in the different categories, with only a few outliers with higher values. The breakdown amonst the lower values can be further observed by reducing the max allowed values in each of the plots using the sliders.

From this data, we know that the majority of planets across the different discovery methods orbit rapidly and close to their stars in relatively circular orbits. However, the overwhelming number of discoveries by the top methods means it's still hard to actually see the differences between the different methods. Thus, a boxplot was created, which can ignore those discovery numbers and just focus on the distribution for each.

With these, the differences in results came into much clearer view. Imaging often found planets with the highest orbital periods, and found those with by far the largest orbital radii. Putting that aside, by radial velocity provided the greatest variation in both orbital period and orbital eccentricity, reaching both some of the highest and lowest values. Pulsar timing, on the other hand, covered the least ground, only detecting planets from a small range of orbital periods, radii, and eccentricities. Astrometry produced higher values than pulsar timing but lower than microlensing in both orbital period and radii, but it was strongly the other way around in regard to eccentricity. In fact, astrometry actually had the highest average eccentricity values out of all the different methods.

But what about the margins of error in those methods? How does that affect these results? We explored this using a set of scatterplots.

The scatterplot was chosen rather than a histogram or boxplot so that the errors could be compared to the measured values they surround rather than just the method type. This let us see that the errors in orbital period generally increased as the orbital period did, but that errors in eccentricity peaked at middling values rather than at the ends. For orbital radii, the radial velocity method did seem to get less precise as radii increased, but it was hard to tell since the imaging method found planets with such high orbital radii and errors that they compressed the other points to one small region of the figure, even with the radii having already been filtered to below a certain maximum value for readability. Imaging also had some high margins of error for eccentricity too, but interestingly was beaten out by microlensing when it came to the size of errors in orbital periods.
